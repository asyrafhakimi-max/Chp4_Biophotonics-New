Topic 1

What is biophotonics?

In simple words

Photonics is the technology of light. Biology is the study of living things. Put them together and you get biophotonics: using light (tiny packets called photons) to look at, test and even treat cells and tissues.

Three big jobs of biophotonics

Imaging

See the shape and structure of cells and tissues.

Diagnosis

Spot changes caused by disease, such as abnormal tissue or disease markers.

Treatment

Use light to treat tissue, such as laser therapy or photodynamic therapy.

Topic 2

Nature of light: a quick revision

In simple words

Light behaves like a wave (a ripple of electric and magnetic fields) and also like a stream of tiny energy packets called photons. The distance between two wave peaks is the wavelength (λ). Short wavelength means a high-energy photon; long wavelength means a low-energy photon.

E = hf = hc / λE photon energy, h Planck’s constant (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s), c speed of light (3.0 × 10⁸ m/s), λ wavelength

UV, about 200–400 nm

High energy. Used to make molecules glow (fluorescence).

Visible, about 400–700 nm

The colours we see. Used for imaging cells and tissue.

Near-infrared, about 700–1000+ nm

Low energy, invisible. Goes deeper into tissue.

Why does wavelength matter? It decides how light behaves in tissue (absorbed, scattered, how deep it goes), which molecules we can light up, and how safe the light is.

Topic 3

How light interacts with biological tissue

In simple words

When light hits your skin, each photon can do one of five things: bounce off, bend as it enters, bump around inside, get swallowed, or pass right through. Which one happens depends a lot on the wavelength.

  • Reflection: light bounces back at the surface (like the shine on skin).
  • Refraction: light bends when it enters a material with a different refractive index (air to tissue).
  • Scattering: light changes direction when it hits tiny structures such as cells and collagen. This makes images blurry.
  • Absorption: molecules such as haemoglobin, water and melanin take up the light energy. This creates image contrast.
  • Transmission: light passes through, but weaker. This decides how deep we can see.

Topic 4

Light absorption and emission

In simple words

Think of an atom as a staircase with only certain steps. An electron sits on the lowest step (the ground state). A photon can lift it to a higher step (the excited state), but only if the photon carries the right amount of energy for that jump. The electron does not stay up long: it drops back down and gives the energy out as a new photon of light.

  • Energy states: atoms and molecules have fixed energy levels, such as the ground state (E₀) and excited state (E₁).
  • Absorption (hν): a photon with suitable energy is taken in and lifts an electron to a higher level.
  • Emission (hν′): the electron falls back and releases the energy as a photon.

Topic 5

How does fluorescence happen?

In simple words

Fluorescence is when a molecule takes in light of one colour, loses a little energy as heat, then gives out light of a different colour. Because some energy is lost, the light that comes out has less energy, so it has a longer wavelength (for example, blue in, green out). It all happens in about a billionth of a second (nanoseconds).

Scientists draw this on a Jablonski diagram: S₀ is the ground state, S₁ and S₂ are excited states. Fluorescence = absorption + relaxation + light emission.

Topic 6

Characteristics of fluorescence

In simple words

Fluorescent light comes out by itself (spontaneous), in all directions, with the waves out of step (incoherent), and at a longer wavelength than the light that went in. The gap between the “in” colour and the “out” colour is called the Stokes shift.

Spontaneous emission

Light is given out naturally when the electron falls back. Nothing triggers it.

Incoherent and multidirectional

Random phase, sent out in many directions, like a light bulb rather than a laser.

Longer emission wavelength

λem > λexc, because some energy is lost as heat first.

Stokes shift

Δλ = λem − λexc. It lets us filter out the excitation light and keep only the glow.

Topic 7

Fluorescence in biomedical applications

In simple words

Cells are mostly see-through, so they are hard to see under normal light. We add fluorescent markers (fluorophores) that stick to one part of the cell only. Under the right light, each part glows its own colour, so we can see exactly where things are.

  • Fluorescence microscopy: imaging biological structures with high contrast.
  • Fluorescent markers: light-absorbing molecules that label specific cells or biomolecules.
  • Uses: cancer cell imaging, pathogen detection, molecular tracking, drug response monitoring, disease diagnosis.

Topic 8

Revision: spontaneous vs stimulated emission

In simple words

Spontaneous emission: an excited electron falls down by itself, whenever it likes, and sends a photon in a random direction. Stimulated emission: a passing photon “nudges” an excited electron, which falls and releases a twin photon: same energy, same direction, same step (phase). Lasers need lots of twins, so they need more atoms excited than relaxed. That is called population inversion, and we get it by pumping energy in.

  • Spontaneous emission is the basis of fluorescence (biophotonics).
  • Stimulated emission + population inversion lead to laser light (Chapter 3).
  • Laser steps: pumping, population inversion, stimulated emission (amplification), laser output that is coherent, intense and one colour.

Topic 9

Fluorescence vs laser emission

In simple words

Fluorescence is like a crowd talking: everyone speaks whenever they like, in every direction. A laser is like a choir: everyone sings the same note, at the same time, facing the same way.

Topic 11

Conventional medical imaging vs optical bioimaging

In simple words

Hospitals use X-ray/CT, MRI and ultrasound to look deep inside the whole body. Optical bioimaging uses ordinary, safe light, so it cannot go as deep, but it shows much smaller details, down to cells and molecules, often in real time and without harmful radiation.

Topic 12

Advantages and limitations of optical bioimaging

In simple words

Light is safe, detailed and fast, but it cannot go very deep (a few millimetres to a few centimetres), and too much light for too long, or the wrong kind such as UV, can still damage tissue.

Advantages

Non-ionising light: safe visible or NIR light.
Cellular and molecular imaging: see cells, organelles, biomolecules.
Dynamic monitoring: watch living processes in real time without destroying them.
Spectroscopic information: fluorescence spectra tell us which molecules are there.

Limitations

Limited tissue penetration: absorption and scattering weaken light within mm to cm.
Optical exposure safety: strong light, long exposure or UV can cause heating or phototoxicity.

Wrap up

Chapter summary

  1. Biophotonics uses light to study, diagnose and treat biological systems.
  2. Fluorescence involves absorption, non-radiative relaxation and spontaneous emission at a longer wavelength.
  3. Laser light differs from fluorescence in its emission mechanism, coherence and directionality.
  4. Optical bioimaging gives valuable structural and molecular information about cells, tissues and biomolecules, but is limited by penetration depth and exposure safety.
